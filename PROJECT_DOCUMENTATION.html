Customer-service triage · Natural language processing

Routing and prioritising financial complaints

A complete account of the system: what problem it solves, what data it learns from, how TF-IDF and DistilBERT are used, which one wins where — and why the project's first version had to be torn down and rebuilt.

1,689,573 CFPB complaints 2 tasks · 4 models Every number traced to metrics.json

01The problem

Two decisions, thousands of times a day, made by reading

A financial institution receives complaints continuously — a card charge nobody recognises, an escrow account miscalculated, a debt collector who will not stop calling. Every one arrives as free text written by an upset human being, and every one needs two decisions before any work can start:

  1. Where does it go? Mortgage servicing cannot resolve a credit-reporting dispute. Route it wrong and the clock restarts when someone finally forwards it.
  2. How urgent is it? Most complaints get an explanation and close. A minority require the company to actually fix something — refund money, correct a record, restore access. Those are the expensive ones, and the ones with regulatory exposure.

Done by hand, both decisions cost a trained person several minutes of reading per ticket, and the second one is the harder call. A complaint that says "I was charged twice" may be a misunderstanding; one that says "I have written three times and nobody has responded" often is not. The difference is not a keyword. It is meaning.

This project automates both decisions from the complaint text alone, and — just as importantly — measures honestly how well that automation actually works.

The second half of that sentence is not boilerplate. This project exists in its current form because its first version got that part badly wrong.

02Why it was rebuilt

The original result was an artifact of its own setup

Version 1 reported that DistilBERT beat a TF-IDF baseline by 24.2 accuracy points on urgency classification, and concluded that transformers were necessary because bag-of-words models cannot understand negation. It was a clean story. It was also not true, for four compounding reasons.

The dataset was 288 sentences wearing a 15,000-row costume

The CSV reported 15,000 records. It contained 288 distinct strings, generated from 24 action phrases × 12 templates × 4 categories, each repeated about 52 times. Under a random train/test split, 100% of test sentences had already been seen verbatim during training. The reported accuracy measured memorisation.

The labels were decided by the template, not the sentence

Urgency was assigned by which template produced the text. Every "Critical" example contained a negation word; no "Low" example did. Zero ambiguity, zero label noise, zero overlap.

The baseline was deliberately handicapped

This is the part that matters most. The urgency baseline was configured with stop_words='english' — and scikit-learn's English stop list contains not, no, cannot, never. Those were precisely the tokens carrying the label. DistilBERT received the same text with those words left in.

The annotation is the notebook's own. The markdown cell above it reads: "Urgency: Will fail (~75%) because we remove stop words." The failure was designed, then reported as a discovery.

Finding

DistilBERT's true advantage over a fairly configured TF-IDF on that data was 0.0 points. The 24.2-point gap was manufactured by deleting the negation words from one model's vocabulary and leaving them in the other's.

Two further problems ran downstream: the confusion matrices in the report were hardcoded arrays rather than computed from predictions, and the dashboard fabricated its own evidence — inventing ticket timestamps with random.randint and hardcoding the $520,000 / 650% ROI figure as a literal list.

Everything that follows is the rebuild. The controls it uses are not stylistic preferences; each one exists because its absence produced a result that was not real.

Practical note

consumerfinance.gov and files.consumerfinance.gov return HTTP 403 from many networks behind the agency's firewall, and the official HuggingFace repo is a loading script that fetches from that blocked host. The parquet mirror is the working path — worth knowing before losing an afternoon to it.

Why this dataset and not a synthetic one

Real complaints bring four things a generated dataset cannot fake, and all four are the point:

  • Genuine ambiguity. Two complaints with near-identical wording can end differently. No model reaches 100%, and that ceiling is real information.
  • Genuine noise. Misspellings, redaction placeholders, legal boilerplate, thousand-word rants — all handled explicitly in §4.
  • Genuine imbalance. One queue is 57% of the corpus; the smallest is under 2%. Accuracy becomes actively misleading, which forces honest metrics.
  • Genuine label drift. The CFPB renamed its own categories mid-stream, and outcome rates shift over time. Both are visible in the results.

Taxonomy consolidation — 21 raw values, 8 real queues

The Product field carries 21 distinct strings, but the CFPB renamed its taxonomy several times without back-filling old records. Three separate strings all mean "credit reporting"; three more all mean "credit card". Training on the raw field asks the model to guess which era a complaint was filed in — that is label noise, not signal.

04Cleaning the noise

Replace the artifacts, never delete them

CFPB narratives are pre-redacted by the agency. Names become XXXX, dates become XX/XX/2023, amounts become {$43.00}. Each needs deliberate handling, and the naive choices are both wrong:

  • Deleting them shreds sentence structure. "I called XXXX on XX/XX/XXXX" becomes "I called on" — the grammar collapses and DistilBERT, which reads word order, loses the thread.
  • Leaving them raw makes redaction density a feature. Longer complaints contain more XXXX, so the model learns to count redactions as a proxy for length instead of reading content.

The resolution: replace each artifact with a single stable token that holds the grammatical slot without carrying spurious signal.

Why company names are scrubbed

The Company field strongly predicts Company response — some firms settle far more often than others. Leaving company names inside the narrative lets the priority model memorise institutions instead of reading complaints. It would score better on test and fail completely on any company it had not seen. Both the row's own company (with corporate suffixes stripped, so "EQUIFAX, INC." matches "Equifax") and a static list of frequently co-mentioned institutions are replaced.

What is deliberately not done

The rule that cannot be relaxed

No stop-word removal. No stemming. No lemmatisation. No lowercasing. No punctuation stripping.

Negation is the signal. Casing carries shouting, punctuation carries legal-citation style, and both are consumed as features. The vectoriser in this codebase raises StopWordRemovalError if anyone passes stop_words — the v1 trap is not merely discouraged, it is unreachable.

One exception, deliberately fenced: the LDA topic model in §13 does remove stop words. Topic modelling is unsupervised description that feeds no reported metric, and function words drown the topics. That is a different job with different rules.

05Deduplication

41% of the corpus was near-duplicate boilerplate

Credit-repair companies file complaints on behalf of clients using template letters. Thousands of consumers submit near-identical text, differing only in the account list, the date, and the redaction pattern. A representative opening, seen over and over in the raw data:

"In accordance with the Fair Credit Reporting act. The List of accounts
 below has violated my federally protected consumer rights to privacy and
 confidentiality under 15 U.S.C. 1681 ..."

Exact-string matching does not catch these — every copy differs slightly. But if they survive into the split, the same letter appears in train and test, and every metric inflates. This is v1's failure, occurring naturally in real data.

Two passes, both run before splitting. Running them afterwards would leave cross-split duplicates untouched — the only case that actually affects a metric.

  1. Exact hash of the narrative with placeholders and digits stripped, so letters differing only in redactions and amounts collapse together.
  2. MinHash LSH at Jaccard ≥ 0.80 over 5-gram word shingles, catching edited copies the hash misses.

07The two tasks

One topical, one semantic — and that distinction is the experiment

The tasks were chosen so that they differ in kind, not just difficulty. That is what makes the TF-IDF vs DistilBERT comparison informative rather than decorative.

Task A · Routing
  • Target: one of 8 support queues
  • Derived from: CFPB Product, consolidated
  • Balance: ~40% Credit Reporting, <1% Student Loan
  • Nature: topical. The word "escrow" means mortgage; "chargeback" means card. Vocabulary largely determines the answer.
  • Headline metric: macro-F1 — every queue counts equally regardless of size
Task B · Priority
  • Target: relief_required, binary
  • Derived from: did the company close with monetary or non-monetary relief?
  • Balance: 34.5% positive on the test window
  • Nature: semantic. No keyword marks it. The same complaint text can resolve either way depending on how the situation is described.
  • Headline metric: PR-AUC — the honest choice under imbalance

Why "relief required" is a defensible priority label

It is a real business outcome recorded by a third party — not a label invented to make the model look good. A complaint that will require remediation deserves a senior handler and a tighter SLA. Predicting it from text alone is genuinely hard, which is the point.

Rows marked In progress or Untimely response are dropped, not coded zero. They are unlabelled, not negative — coding them as negatives would teach the model that unresolved cases are low priority, which inverts the truth.

A caveat that belongs up front

The label records how a complaint was resolved, not whether the consumer was right. Company behaviour and the CFPB's own processes shape it. The model predicts institutional outcome, and that is what it should be trusted to do — nothing more.

08TF-IDF

Counting words, weighted by how surprising they are

What it is

TF-IDF turns a document into a long vector of numbers, one slot per vocabulary term. Two factors multiply:

  • Term Frequency — how often the term appears in this document. A complaint saying "escrow" five times is probably about escrow.
  • Inverse Document Frequency — how rare the term is across the whole corpus. "the" appears everywhere and carries nothing; "escrow" appears in a small subset and is highly informative. IDF is the log of that inverse ratio, so common terms are damped and rare ones amplified.

The result: each complaint becomes a point in a space with hundreds of thousands of dimensions, where distance approximates topical similarity. A linear classifier then finds hyperplanes separating the classes.

How it is configured here

word  1–2 grams, 200,000 features   # topical content + negation bigrams
char_wb 3–5 grams, 100,000 features  # robustness to misspellings
min_df = 3, sublinear_tf = True
stop_words = None                    # NOT CONFIGURABLE — raises if passed
        ↓
LinearSVC(C=1.0, class_weight="balanced")

Four decisions worth explaining:

  • Bigrams, because unigrams cannot represent negation. "cannot pay" and "can pay" share both unigrams; only the bigram distinguishes them. This is the single fix that dissolves v1's entire premise.
  • Character n-grams, because real consumer text is full of misspellings — "recieved", "unathorized", "creidt". Word features miss those entirely; character 3–5 grams still match most of the surrounding letters. On v1's clean synthetic text this mattered not at all; here it matters a great deal.
  • sublinear_tf applies log scaling, so a word repeated twenty times in a rant does not dominate twenty-fold.
  • class_weight="balanced", because Student Loan is under 1% of rows and would otherwise be ignored entirely.

Why LinearSVC

With hundreds of thousands of sparse features, linear models are both fast and strong — the classes are usually close to linearly separable in that space. It also gives directly readable coefficients: you can ask which terms pushed a prediction, which matters in a regulated setting. For Task B it is wrapped in CalibratedClassifierCV, because SVMs output decision distances rather than probabilities, and PR-AUC needs calibrated scores.

The fundamental limitation

Order blindness

TF-IDF is a bag of words. "The bank charged me" and "I charged the bank" produce identical vectors. Bigrams patch this locally — they capture two-word order — but nothing connects a word at position 4 to one at position 60. When meaning depends on long-range structure, TF-IDF cannot see it. That is exactly where Task B lives.

09DistilBERT

Reading words in the context of every other word

What it is

BERT is a transformer: a neural network that represents each word as a vector shaped by all the other words around it. The mechanism is self-attention — for every word, the model computes how much each other word should influence its representation. "Charged" in "charged me twice" and "charged with fraud" get different vectors, because attention pulls in different neighbours.

This is the concrete difference from TF-IDF. TF-IDF gives "charged" one fixed slot regardless of context. BERT gives it a different vector every time, computed from the sentence it sits in.

Bidirectional means it reads left and right simultaneously — pretrained by masking random words and predicting them from both sides. DistilBERT is a compressed version: 6 transformer layers instead of 12, trained to mimic full BERT's outputs. It keeps roughly 97% of the language understanding at 40% smaller and 60% faster.

Transfer learning — why this works at all

DistilBERT arrives already knowing English, learned from billions of words of general text. Fine-tuning does not teach it language; it teaches it this task, by adding a small classification head on top of the [CLS] summary token and updating all weights on 200,000 labelled complaints. That is why two epochs suffice where training from scratch would need vastly more data.

How it is configured here

ParameterValueWhy
max_length256Covers roughly the median complaint. Attention costs scale quadratically — 512 would roughly triple epoch time
batch_size32Fits a T4's 16GB with fp16
learning_rate3e-5Standard fine-tuning range; higher destroys pretrained weights
num_epochs2More overfits, especially with residual boilerplate
warmup_ratio0.06Ramps LR gently so early batches do not wreck the weights
weight_decay0.01Regularisation
fp16trueHalf precision — roughly 2× faster on a T4
lossweighted CEClass-weighted cross-entropy; mandatory on the imbalanced target

Dynamic padding pads each batch to its own longest sequence rather than a fixed 256, which is roughly 30% faster given most complaints are shorter.

Honest limitation

256 tokens is about 190 words. The training corpus averages 195 words and reaches 5,791. A real tail of long complaints is truncated — the model never sees their endings. This belongs in the limitations section, not hidden in a config file.

The decision rule

The choice is not "which is better". It is what kind of problem is this:

Reach for TF-IDF when
  • The label is determined by which words appear — topic, category, department, tag
  • You need CPU inference, or millisecond latency at volume
  • You must explain a decision to an auditor or regulator
  • You are establishing a baseline — always do this first
  • Retraining must be cheap and frequent
Reach for DistilBERT when
  • The label depends on how words relate — intent, sentiment, escalation, outcome
  • Negation, hedging, or conditionals change the meaning
  • The same vocabulary appears in both classes
  • A measured baseline has already shown TF-IDF is insufficient
  • You have a GPU and the accuracy gain is worth the cost

Task A is the first column. Task B is the second. This project runs both models on both tasks precisely so the prediction can be checked rather than asserted.

The difference is not statistically significant. DistilBERT gains 0.009 macro-F1 for 5× the training time and 10× the model size. The confidence intervals overlap substantially.

DistilBERT wins, significantly. +0.030 PR-AUC and +0.047 ROC-AUC over TF-IDF, and it got 1,075 test rows right that TF-IDF missed against 567 the other way.

Both models clear the keyword floor by a wide margin — 0.53 and 0.56 against 0.38 — so the task genuinely requires reading, not matching.

This is the result v1 claimed to have found, obtained honestly. Contextual understanding earns its cost on the semantic task and only there. The measured ROC-AUC also lands almost exactly where the published literature on this dataset sits (TF-IDF ≈ 0.69, stronger hybrid models ≈ 0.78), which is a good sign the pipeline is behaving.

Reading the operating point

Thresholds were tuned on validation and applied unchanged to test. TF-IDF settles at 0.176 — it casts a wide net (81% recall, 45% precision). DistilBERT settles at 0.481 and is more discriminating (72% recall, 53% precision). For triage, high recall is usually the right trade: missing a complaint that needed remediation costs more than a second look at one that did not. The threshold is a policy dial, not a fixed property of the model.

Defaults resolve to the measured recommendation — TF-IDF routing, DistilBERT priority — and are overridable per request so the dashboard's comparison view can run all four combinations. A complaint whose cleaned text falls below the 40-character training floor is rejected with HTTP 422 rather than answered: the models have no basis for it, and a confident-looking number there is precisely what this project exists not to produce.

Policy is kept separate from prediction. Team, SLA, and escalation bands live in backend/routing.py, and nothing there is learned from data. v1's dashboard blurred exactly this line, presenting a hardcoded ROI beside real model output as though both were measured.

Guardrails enforced in code, not convention

  • features.StopWordRemovalError — the vectoriser refuses the argument that fabricated v1's result
  • dataset.py exposes temporal_split and no random alternative
  • evaluate.assert_not_suspicious — fails any run with a metric ≥ 0.99
  • test_v1_metrics_would_have_been_rejected — a unit test asserting v1's published numbers fail the build today
  • TestFrontendIsolation — the dashboard may not import torch, transformers, sklearn or joblib, and may not contain a hardcoded metric literal. Comments and docstrings may name v1's fabricated figures, because describing the failure is the point

84 tests. Backend and frontend verified running against the trained artifacts on CPU: routing ~0.5 s per complaint end to end, including DistilBERT priority.

One public link, free hosting. streamlit_app.py runs the API in a daemon thread on 127.0.0.1:8000 and serves the dashboard on the public port, so the split above survives on hosts that give you a single process — the dashboard still speaks HTTP and still imports no ML library.

CFPB-8127870 — where the label is arguably wrong

Filed under Debt Collection, but the narrative is a credit-report dispute sent to a credit bureau about charge-off entries. Both models answer Credit Reporting and are scored wrong.

Read it and the models have the better of the argument. CFPB product categories genuinely overlap, and some of the residual routing error in §12 is label noise, not model error. A demo that only showed wins would never surface this.

The page reports its own limits

Running all twenty gives 14/20 routing and 13/20 priority — displayed alongside an explicit warning that this is a deliberately hard, failure-weighted bank and not an accuracy estimate. The measured numbers, on all 5,777 test complaints, are in §12.

A test asserts the stored predictions still reproduce against the loaded artifacts, so a bank left stale after retraining fails the build rather than quietly misleading a reader.

It also works on anything you type

The system is not keyed to its examples. Complaints written fresh — an auto repossession despite a payment plan, a stalled PSLF certification, an incorrect mortgage payoff statement, a settled medical bill still in collections — each route to the queue a person would pick, and that is asserted in the test suite.

Off-domain text is answered anyway, because the model has no none of the above class; the interface says so plainly rather than letting a confident wrong answer pass unremarked. Narratives under 40 characters after cleaning are refused with HTTP 422 — the models were trained only on longer text, and a confident number there is exactly the failure this project was rebuilt to avoid.

17How it helps

What the numbers mean for a support operation

Concretely, for each incoming complaint the system returns a queue with a confidence score, a relief-required probability, and a routing recommendation with an SLA.

Routing

Macro-F1 0.73 against a 0.07 floor. Roughly three in four complaints land in the right queue without a human reading them. That is not a replacement for triage staff — it is a strong first pass with a confidence score, so low-confidence cases can be flagged for review while high-confidence ones route automatically.

Priority

At the chosen operating point DistilBERT catches 72% of complaints that will require remediation, at 53% precision. In practice: about half of what it flags genuinely needs senior attention, and it finds roughly three-quarters of the cases that do. Against a base rate of 34.5%, that is a substantial lift over reading them in arrival order.

The deployment recommendation the evidence supports

Recommended configuration

TF-IDF for routing. DistilBERT for priority.

Routing gets a 30 MB CPU model with millisecond inference and readable coefficients, at no measurable quality cost. Priority gets the 257 MB transformer, because there the accuracy is real and worth the GPU. A single architecture for both would be worse on one of the two axes.

This mixed recommendation is only possible because both models were run on both tasks and the difference was tested rather than assumed. Had the project reported only the headline it hoped for, it would have deployed a transformer for routing and paid 5× the cost for statistical noise.

18Limitations

Stated plainly, because the alternative is how v1 happened

  • The test set is small. 5,777 rows from a five-week window, because the mirror's coverage thins after January 2024 and deduplication removed 41%. Confidence intervals are correspondingly wide (±0.02 on macro-F1) and are reported everywhere.
  • Substantial temporal drift. Relief rate rises 19.8% → 29.5% → 34.5% across train, validation and test. The models are calibrated on a world where relief was rarer than in the test window, which likely understates achievable performance and definitely means thresholds need periodic recalibration in production.
  • 256-token truncation. Roughly 190 words, against a corpus averaging 195 and reaching 5,791. Long complaints are cut off; the model never sees their conclusions.
  • The label is an outcome, not a judgement. relief_required records what the company did, shaped by company policy and CFPB process — not whether the consumer was right. The model should never be described as detecting valid complaints.
  • English-only, US-only, web-submitted. Every narrative in the has-text split arrived through the CFPB web form. Phone and mail complaints are absent, and that population may differ.
  • Consent bias. Narratives are published only when the consumer opts in. Consumers who consent may not represent all complainants.
  • Small queues remain weak. Money Transfer & Virtual Currency reaches only 0.52 F1 on 137 test rows. Both models struggle there; more data is the fix, not a different architecture.
  • Residual boilerplate. Shingle overlap of 0.147 between train and test. Exact leakage is zero, but complaints share ordinary legal phrasing and deduplication cannot remove that without destroying real data.
The standard this project holds itself to

Every number on this page traces to a key in models/metrics.json, produced by notebook 02 from actual predictions. No confusion matrix is typed in by hand. No ROI figure is invented. Where a result is negative — as Task A's is — it is reported as a negative result.

That is the entire difference between this version and the one it replaced.
