Why it was done this way

Rules decide which alarms matter — and the data decided the rules

Measured on the live mirror (4–16 Oct): 932 alarms; "Cashless device" 20 a day, 95 % gone in under 5 minutes; "Communication Interrupted" 20 a day, 90 %; while "No cash sales" lingers a median 455 minutes and "Product level low" 305. ISA-18.2's on-delay is the standard tool: wait a set time before calling an alarm a problem. Nothing about who or whether is left to a model.

Names lie; the data does not

The obvious refinement — ignore cash alarms on machines named "card only" — would have hidden real faults: those machines sold 2,619 cash cups in 14 days, and every open cash alarm was on a machine that had taken cash (G193). The rules stay per alarm, not per name.

Switched off until someone is named

With the demo's catch-all routing every problem goes to the CEO. Thirty pages a day to the CEO is how alerting gets ignored, so the pipeline is built, tested, and off; the command centre lists the same alarms meanwhile.

"Should have found", not "is missing"

Televend's own cash sales say what a collection should find; on 16 Oct 18 of 29 reported collections matched exactly and 11 reported more (VF75). The system states the expectation and leaves the judgement to a person — a custody fact, never a score.

How it works

Televend read (every 10 min)→tv_event→alarm_rule: wait · raise · ignore→ machine_alarm → blocker (system actor)→routeAndAlert: resolver, SLA, page→sla_sweep → escalation
expected cash (collection c) = count(vends: payment_type = 0, at ∈ [previous collection + 1 min, c + 1 min)) × coalesce(machine.cash_price_fils, AED 10)
state = offline (connection 0) › needs someone (an alarm past its on-delay) › Televend warning › OK
