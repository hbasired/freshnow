UAE data residency, the PDPL, and where our AI should run

Prepared 2026-09-19 for FreshNow (Daily Vending LLC, Dubai) · researched today against primary sources where they exist · every claim carries a confidence marker

How to read this. I am not a lawyer and this is not legal advice; it is a technical analysis of the law as written and of what our system actually does, so that a UAE data-protection lawyer can confirm it quickly. Markers: [verified] read in a primary source (the law's text, a vendor's own documentation, our own database); [believed] from reputable secondary sources that agree with each other; [uncertain] sources conflict or I could not find one — verify before relying on it; [assumed] a fact about FreshNow I do not have. Three claims that appeared in search summaries turned out to be wrong when I checked the primary page (noted in §8) — which is why the markers matter.

1 · The short answers

Your questionAnswer
Is it true that personal data of employees or customers must not leave the UAE? Not as a general rule. [verified] The federal Personal Data Protection Law (Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021, "PDPL") does not require personal data to be stored in the UAE. It regulates transfer abroad (Articles 22–23): allowed to countries the regulator deems adequate, or otherwise under a contract, with the person's express consent, or where necessary for a contract with them — quoted in §2. Strict in-country storage rules do exist, but for specific sectors [believed]: health data (Federal Law 2/2019, the "ICT Health Law"), banking and payment data (Central Bank rules), and government data. FreshNow's operational employee data is none of those [assumed: Daily Vending LLC is a mainland company, not DIFC/ADGM]. The complication: the PDPL's Executive Regulations — which are supposed to set the adequacy list, the contract templates and the transfer procedure — had not been issued as of March 2026 [believed — Chambers Global Practice Guide, 10 Mar 2026], so the "adequate country" route does not yet exist in practice and companies are relying on consent and contracts.
Does our system send personal data out of the country today? Yes — in four ways [verified from our own database and code]: (1) every Telegram message transits Telegram's servers, which are outside the UAE; (2) employees' free-text updates, colleagues' names, end-of-day summaries, query rows and one voice recording have been sent to Groq (250 chat calls + 1 Whisper), NVIDIA (54) and OpenRouter (17) — all US companies with no UAE processing; (3) call metadata (never content) goes to Langfuse in Ireland; (4) the production plan puts the whole database on a Hostinger VPS, and Hostinger has no data centre in the UAE or anywhere in the Middle East [verified on Hostinger's own locations page]. Today the database is on a laptop in Dubai, so (4) has not happened yet — it is the single biggest thing to change before go-live. Full inventory in §4.
Is consent enough? On paper, express consent is one of the lawful grounds for transfer (Art. 23(1)(b)) [verified]. In practice I would not rely on it alone, for four reasons: our current notice does not tell employees their words go to an AI service abroad, that voice notes are transcribed abroad, or that managers now see their reports' data — so today's consent does not cover today's processing [verified — the notice text is quoted in §5]; consent is withdrawable at any time (Art. 6(2)), so the system must keep working lawfully when it is withdrawn; the Executive Regulations may add conditions; and the transfer to an AI provider also needs a processor contract (Art. 7(5), Art. 8) and a record of cross-border processing (Art. 7(4)) regardless of consent. The robust position is: fix the notice, sign processor contracts, and move processing in-country so that consent is a belt, not the braces.
We only collect employees' work-related data — is that good enough? It helps a lot, and it is not an exemption. Work data about an identifiable person is personal data; the PDPL applies fully. What the narrow scope buys you: data minimisation and purpose limitation (Art. 5) are largely satisfied by design; the employment and contract grounds in Art. 4(8)–(9) give a lawful basis for the processing itself beyond consent [verified]; and because we deliberately do no scoring, ranking or profiling, the mandatory impact assessment of Art. 21 (profiling with legal effects) is not triggered [believed]. Two cautions: free text and voice can carry sensitive data ("I was off sick", a medical reason for a blocker) that the law treats more strictly — the extraction prompt should be told to ignore health content and the retention window should be set; and the Labour Law (Federal Decree-Law 33/2021) expects monitoring to be disclosed in the contract or a written policy [believed].
Is calling LLM APIs the way we do now correct? Technically yes; legally it is the weakest link. An AI provider that receives employee text is a processor under the PDPL, and the law requires the controller to appoint processors "who provide sufficient guarantees" under a contract (Art. 7(5), Art. 8(1)) and to keep a record of the cross-border processing (Art. 7(4)) [verified]. We use Groq, NVIDIA and OpenRouter on consumer-grade API terms with no data-processing agreement, and none of them processes in the UAE. The fix is not "bring the model in-house". The fix is "use a model that runs in the UAE, under a contract, with less personal data in the prompt." Those exist today — §6.
Should we self-host an open model and train it on company data to match the 120B model we use? No, on all three counts, and the numbers are decisive: the model we use (openai/gpt-oss-120b) needs about 61 GB just for its weights [verified — llama.cpp's gpt-oss guide]; the VPS has 32 GB and no GPU, so it cannot load. The 20B sibling (12 GB) would load, but CPU-only generation on eight shared vCPUs would starve Postgres — the exact failure CLAUDE.md forbids — and it is a weaker model for Hindi/Malayalam extraction. "Training on company data" is the wrong tool: our tasks are extraction, translation-to-SQL and summarising, which are solved by prompts and the semantic layer, not weights; we have a handful of real free-text rows, nowhere near a fine-tuning set; and training data would itself have to be sent somewhere. What to do instead: the same gpt-oss-120b is offered under UAE jurisdiction by Core42 (a G42 company) at $0.15–0.25 per million input tokens [verified on Core42's page]; OpenAI itself now offers a UAE endpoint with in-region processing for selected models, by approval [verified — OpenAI developer docs]; Azure UAE North runs Whisper in-region on pay-as-you-go [verified — Microsoft's region table]. Cost comparison and the code change in §6.

2 · What the law actually says (primary text)

Quotations are from the English translation of Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 published by LexisNexis Middle East (PDF); the official Arabic text is on uaelegislation.gov.ae (the site refused automated access, so I could not compare the two). The law came into force on 2 January 2022 [believed].

Scope — it applies to us [verified]

Article 2(1): "The provisions of this Decree Law shall apply to the Processing of Personal Data … by: … (b) any Controller or Processor located in the State who carries out the activities of Processing Personal Data of Data Subjects inside or outside the State."

Excluded (Art. 2(2)): government data, health data and banking/credit data that have their own laws, and "companies and institutions located in the free zones of the State and are subject to special legislation on Personal Data Protection" — i.e. DIFC and ADGM have their own regimes. If Daily Vending LLC is licensed in DIFC or ADGM this whole analysis changes [assumed mainland].

Lawful basis — consent is the default, employment and contract are exceptions [verified]

Article 4: "It is prohibited to process Personal Data without the consent of the Data Subject. However, the following cases … are excluded from such prohibition: … (8) if the Processing is necessary for the Controller or Data Subject to fulfill his/her obligations and exercise his/her legally established rights in the field of employment, social security or laws on social protection, to the extent permitted by those laws. (9) if the Processing is necessary to perform a contract to which the Data Subject is a party …"
Article 6: consent must be provable, "given in a clear, simple, unambiguous and easily accessible manner", must "indicate the right of the Data Subject to withdraw it", and "the Data Subject may, at any time, withdraw his/her Consent".

Principles — minimisation, purpose, retention, security [verified]

Article 5: "Personal Data must be sufficient for and limited to the purpose for which the Processing is made" … "Personal Data may not be kept after fulfilling the purpose of Processing thereof. It may only be kept in the event that the identity of the Data Subject is anonymized …"

Controller and processor duties — the ones that bite us [verified]

Article 7: the Controller shall "(2) apply the appropriate measures … including Pseudonymization"; "(4) maintain a special record of Personal Data which must include … the purpose of Processing and any data related to the movement and Cross-Border Processing of such data"; "(5) appoint a Processor who provides sufficient guarantees to apply technical and organizational measures …".
Article 8(1): the Processor shall "make and carry out the Processing in accordance with the instructions of the Controller and the contracts and agreements concluded between them that specify in particular the scope, subject, purpose and nature of the Processing, the type of Personal Data and categories of Data Subjects."
Article 9: the Controller shall, "immediately upon becoming aware of any infringement or breach … report such infringement or breach … to the Office within such period … as set by the Executive Regulations".
Article 15(2): the right to request erasure, including "(b) if the Data Subject withdraws his/her Consent on which the Processing is based."
Article 20(1): security measures "which may include … (a) encryption of Personal Data and application of Pseudonymization."
Article 21(2): an impact assessment is required "(a) if the Processing involves a systematic and comprehensive assessment of the personal aspects of the Data Subject based on Automated Processing, including Profiling …; (b) if the Processing will be made on a large amount of Sensitive Personal Data."

Cross-border transfer — the two articles this whole question turns on [verified]

Article 22: "Personal Data may be transferred outside the State in the following cases approved by the Office: (1) if the country or territory to which the Personal Data is to be transferred has special legislation on Personal Data Protection … (2) if the State accedes to bilateral or multilateral agreements related to Personal Data Protection with the countries to which the Personal Data is to be transferred."
Article 23(1): "With the exception of what is stated in Article (22) hereof, Personal Data may be transferred outside the State in the following cases: (a) … under a contract or agreement that obliges the Establishment in those countries to implement the provisions, measures, controls and requirements set out herein …; (b) The express Consent of the Data Subject to transfer his/her Personal Data outside the State in a manner that does not conflict with the security and public interest of the State; (c) … judicial authorities; (d) If the transfer is necessary to enter into or execute a contract between the Controller and Data Subject …; (e) … international judicial cooperation; (f) … public interest." Article 23(2): "The Executive Regulations of this Decree Law shall set the controls and requirements for the cases referred to in Item (1) …"

The Executive Regulations — still missing, which is the real-world problem [believed]

Article 28: "The Cabinet shall … issue the Executive Regulations of this Decree Law within six (6) months from the date of its promulgation." Article 29: "Controllers and Processors shall regularize their status … within a period not exceeding six (6) months from the date of issuance of its Executive Regulations."

The six months ran out in 2022. The Chambers Data Protection & Privacy 2026 UAE chapter (10 March 2026) states: "The Implementing Regulations, intended to clarify key aspects of the law, have yet to be issued" and "the Data Office has not yet become fully operational", with the TDRA acting as contact in the interim. Clifford Chance (10 March 2025) said the same and added that the UAE PDPL "does not impose a general data localisation requirement". Several 2026 compliance vendors state that no adequacy list and no standard contractual clauses have been published. Consequence for us: the adequacy route (Art. 22) is not usable; the practical routes are consent (23(1)(b)), contract necessity (23(1)(d)) and a processor contract that imports the law's requirements (23(1)(a) — whose wording, "countries where there is no data protection law", is odd for the US and EU; a lawyer should say how it is read). [uncertain] I could not find a primary source showing the Regulations were issued between March and September 2026. One search summary asserted a "2026 publication"; the page it pointed at actually says "in progress". Check the Official Gazette and the UAE Data Office before go-live; the regularisation clock starts the day they are issued.

Sector localisation rules (they do not apply to FreshNow's ops data, but you should know they exist) [believed]

  • Health — Federal Law No. 2 of 2019 (ICT in Health Fields), Art. 13: health information may not be stored, processed or transferred outside the UAE except in cases the Ministry permits. Relevant only if we ever record health-related content — which is a reason to keep it out of free-text extraction.
  • Banking and payments — Central Bank Consumer Protection Standards and the Retail Payment Services regulation require customer and payment data to be kept in the UAE. Relevant to the Phase 5 customer channel and any card data — never to be stored in this system.
  • Government — Cloud First / TDRA sovereign-cloud requirements apply to government workloads, not to a private LLC.
  • Free zones — DIFC Data Protection Law 5/2020 and ADGM Data Protection Regulations 2021 have their own transfer rules and adequacy lists; the DIFC has also amended its rules for autonomous/AI systems (Chambers 2026).

3 · Our situation in one picture

Today (demo on a laptop in Dubai): data at rest is in the UAE. Data in motion leaves it through Telegram (unavoidable with any consumer messenger), through three US AI providers (avoidable), and as metadata to Langfuse in Ireland (avoidable, already planned to self-host).

The production plan as written (Hostinger KVM 8): data at rest would be in Lithuania, the Netherlands, the UK, France, Germany, India, Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, the USA or Brazil — Hostinger's list — because Hostinger offers no UAE location. That would turn every row of the employee database into a permanent cross-border transfer, with no adequacy decision to rely on. It is also the easiest thing on this page to fix: choose a host with a UAE region.

5 · The consent notice we show today does not describe today's system [verified]

This is the text every employee agrees to (English; Hindi and Malayalam say the same and are marked UNVERIFIED in the code as not reviewed by a native speaker):

"Before you start — what this bot records: • The daily task status you report (task, status, and your own words). • Blockers you raise, so they reach the person who can clear them. What it does NOT do: • No location tracking. No productivity scoring. No sentiment analysis. Your updates are stored in the company database, visible to you and the CEO. You can withdraw consent at any time with /withdraw."

Three things are now untrue or missing: (1) since the org model, a manager or department lead also sees their reports' updates; (2) the words are sent to an AI service outside the UAE to be understood; (3) voice notes are transcribed by an AI service outside the UAE. Nothing about retention or the right to erasure either. Under Art. 6 consent must be clear and specific to what is done, and under Art. 5(1) processing must be transparent — so the consent we hold today does not cover the transfer we make today. The system already stores a hash of the exact notice text and a policy version, so a new notice is a new version, not a rewrite of history — every existing employee should be asked again.

Draft notice v2 (for the company and its lawyer to approve — not deployed):
"Before you start — what this bot records: your daily task status (task, status, your own words), blockers you raise, and voice notes if you send them. Who sees it: you, your manager or department lead, and the CEO. How it is understood: your words and voice notes are read by an AI service to detect problems and route them; that service runs in [the UAE / country], under a contract with the company, and does not keep your words. It never scores, ranks or monitors you. Kept for: your words for [N] days, then removed; task records stay. Your rights: see, correct or erase your data, and withdraw consent at any time with /withdraw — the company will then stop processing your words and anonymise your record." Have the Hindi and Malayalam versions translated by a native speaker before use.

Why not fine-tune a model on company data?

  • The tasks do not need it. Extracting {status, blocker, category, severity} from a sentence, writing SQL against our semantic layer, summarising a day — these are what instruction-tuned models already do; the company knowledge lives in semantic/schema.yaml, the routing table and the prompts, all of which the company can edit without touching any model.
  • There is nothing to train on. Fine-tuning needs thousands of labelled examples; the live database holds seven employees and a few dozen real updates. A model tuned on that would be worse, not better.
  • It would not answer the residency question — the training data would have to be uploaded somewhere, and serving the result needs the GPU we do not have.
  • "As good as the 120B model" is not achievable on 32 GB of CPU RAM by any method; the 120B model itself is what you get from Core42 for a quarter of a dollar per million tokens.

Cost reality [verified from our own cost log]

322 model calls to date cost $0.0076 in total. At Core42's published rate for the same model the same volume would cost about the same order of magnitude — cents, not dollars. Even at 100× the demo volume, model spend is a rounding error next to one UAE-hosted server. The decision is about residency and contracts, not money.

The code change is small [verified against the wrapper's design]

Every model call already goes through one wrapper with a configurable provider order (LLM_PROVIDER_ORDER=groq,openrouter,nvidia today) and a budget circuit-breaker that degrades to rules-only. The change is: add a provider entry whose base URL is the UAE-hosted endpoint, put it first, and remove the foreign providers from the fallback list so that an outage degrades to rules-only + human review (which the system already supports: needs_review) rather than silently routing employee text abroad. Then re-run the existing contract tests and the model benchmark against the new endpoint before switching. Voice: point the transcription URL at Azure UAE North Whisper (or Core42's transcribe) — one URL and one key.

  • Whether the Executive Regulations have been issued since March 2026. Chambers (10 Mar 2026) says no. I could not reach the UAE Data Office or the legislation portal by automated fetch. Verify with counsel.
  • Google Cloud "has a UAE region" — a search summary said so; Google's own locations page lists only Dammam (Saudi Arabia) in the Middle East. It does not.
  • "Claude is available in-region in me-central-1" — a search summary said so; AWS's region table, quoted verbatim, shows Claude as Global-only there, with Amazon Nova Pro and Nova Lite as the in-region models.
  • Core42's logging and retention policy — their pages say "under UAE jurisdiction" and mention zero customer data logging in marketing; I did not find the contractual statement. Get their DPA before sending employee text.
  • OpenAI's UAE pricing uplift and eligibility come from a search summary of a help-centre article that refused automated access; the region and model list come from OpenAI's developer docs directly.
  • Whether Daily Vending LLC is a mainland or free-zone entity, and whether it has a UAE data-protection lawyer. Both change the advice.
  • CPU-only speed of gpt-oss-20b on this VPS class. Not measured; the recommendation does not depend on it because the model would compete with Postgres regardless.
  • The Hindi and Malayalam notices were machine-written and are flagged in the code as unreviewed by a native speaker.
