The answer in one slide

Four questions, four answers

1 · Can we host the database and system on Hostinger?

Yes, it can be lawful. The PDPL has no rule forcing employee data to stay in the UAE. But every record on Hostinger is a transfer abroad, and today only two grounds work: each employee's express consent, and a contract that imposes the law's controls — whose use for an EU country is unsettled. So: an EU data centre, consent (built — notice 2.0), Hostinger's DPA accepted, and the security controls on slide 6. No extra contract has to be signed — the DPA is part of Hostinger's Terms — but file it, and ask Hostinger in writing to confirm it covers your account and where the backups live (slide 8). [believed — counsel to confirm]

2 · Can we keep Groq or OpenRouter?

For code or anonymous text: yes, freely. For employee words: Groq yes, with consent (built), Zero Data Retention switched on, its DPA filed, and names and numbers removed first — its contract already forbids training on our data (slide 9). OpenRouter no for employee words on a normal account: its DPA applies to enterprise accounts only, so there is no processor contract to rely on. Both process in the US. [believed — slides 7 and 9]

3 · Can "policy as code" make sure there is no violation?

It can make known violations impossible to deploy or to run — an AI provider nobody registered, a message to someone who has not agreed, retention unset, backups unencrypted — and it produces the evidence. It cannot sign a DPA, decide what the law means, or see inside Hostinger. "No violation" is a legal conclusion: counsel draws it, code enforces it and proves it. [slides 10–13]

4 · What do we recommend?

A UAE host + Core42 for the AI. A Dubai VPS costs $27.70–$52.70 a month; Hostinger's KVM 8 is $29.99 the first term, then $49.99. The saving from Hostinger is at most a few dollars a month, against a permanent transfer of the whole database that rests on consent any employee can withdraw. If the company still chooses Hostinger: Option B, with every condition on slide 6. [prices believed, 19–26 Sep]

Where enforcement stands (re-checked 30 Sep 2026)

  • Executive Regulations: still pending (Ashurst, July 2026; Morgan Lewis, June 2026: "have still not been issued") [believed]
  • Adequacy list and contract templates: not issued [believed — ABS Partners, Kayrouz 2026]
  • A Federal Authority for AI and Data exists since 14 June 2026 and absorbed the Data Office; the market expects it to finish the Regulations [believed — Morgan Lewis and three other reports]
  • Six search claims that named an "issued" Regulations instrument have now been traced and found unsupported. The sixth (30 Sep): a compliance-vendor page calling "Cabinet Decision No. 33 of 2024" the Executive Regulation — a search for that number returned only other 2024 Cabinet Resolutions on the official legislation portal, and the June–July 2026 law-firm updates above say nothing has been issued [believed — treated as false]. This is why every legal status here carries a marker.

What that means for choosing a host

Nothing forbids Hostinger today and nobody is auditing today. But a choice made now has to survive the day the Regulations arrive — they may add procedures for exactly the transfers Hostinger would require, with six months to comply. A UAE host is the only choice those Regulations cannot touch.

If Hostinger: which data centre

Hostinger's VPS locations: France, Germany, Lithuania, UK, India, Indonesia, Malaysia, USA (Phoenix, Boston), Brazil — none in the UAE or the Middle East. [verified 21 Sep on Hostinger's page] [re-checked 26 Sep and 29 Sep via search]

The weakness of consent as the only ground

Consent can be withdrawn at any time, and in an employment relationship whether it is freely given is contested. A person who says no must still be able to work — without the system, or through a UAE-only path we have not built. With a UAE host the database needs no ground at all; only Telegram does.

Policy as code · its limits

What no amount of code can promise

It cannot sign or negotiate

Accepting Hostinger's DPA, asking for UAE clauses, getting Core42's terms in writing — people do these. The registry only refuses to proceed until they are recorded.

It cannot decide the law

Whether consent from an employee is valid, or whether Art. 23(1)(a) covers an EU country, is for counsel. Code enforces the answer once it exists.

It cannot see inside Hostinger

Their staff, their sub-processors, the machine under our VPS. The DPA and encryption we hold the keys to are the only controls there.

It cannot stop hand copying

A screenshot, a forwarded Telegram message, an export. That is policy, training and access roles.

It cannot foresee the Regulations

When they arrive, the rules change. The registry makes that a one-file change, re-checked and re-consented automatically.

What it does guarantee

That a known rule is never silently broken by a deploy or a configuration change — and that every claim of compliance can be shown from the database.

So "without any violation" is three things together: counsel's reading of the law · the company's contracts and decisions · code that enforces both and keeps the evidence. Code alone is one of three.

  5. Registry (built): set the Hostinger entry and hosting_countries_allowed to the EU; pnpm compliance --production must pass.
  6. Decide the path for anyone who does not consent.
Either way, one thing is already done: consent is asked, enforced and recorded in Telegram and the app, and it follows the configuration automatically. What remains is a choice of host, a few contracts, and the registry that makes every future change safe.
